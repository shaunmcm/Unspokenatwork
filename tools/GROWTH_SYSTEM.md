* Emotional storytelling that makes employees feel understood.
* Psychological curiosity that encourages viewers to keep watching.
* Practical insights employees want to save.
* Controversial but defensible workplace opinions that generate genuine discussion.

SECTION 2 — THE 30-DAY GROWTH MISSION

PRIMARY OBJECTIVE:

Reach 10,000 total Instagram followers within 30 days.

SECONDARY OBJECTIVES:

1. Consistently exceed 200 organic views per Reel, then grow toward 1,000, 5,000, 10,000, and 100,000+ views.
2. Increase reach among people who do not already follow the page.
3. Maximize meaningful shares and saves.
4. Turn profile visitors into followers.
5. Create original content people actively anticipate.
6. Build a recognizable account that can eventually support ethical monetization.

Treat 10,000 followers as an ambitious performance target, not a promised result.

At the beginning, determine my current follower count and calculate the number of followers needed to reach 10,000.

Each day, recalculate:

* Current follower count.
* Net followers gained.
* Average daily follower growth.
* Days remaining.
* Daily net followers required to reach 10,000.
* Projected 30-day total at the current growth rate.

If performance is below target, improve the strategy through evidence-based experimentation.

Do not respond to weak performance by blindly producing more low-quality posts.

SECTION 3 — ADAPTIVE DAILY CONTENT VOLUME

IMPORTANT:

Do not operate with a permanent fixed number of posts.

I authorize an adaptive publishing strategy that increases or decreases content volume according to quality, audience response, analytics, and platform restrictions.

STARTING DAILY TEST SCHEDULE:

* 2 original Reels.
* 1 original carousel.
* 5–8 Story frames, distributed into useful sequences.

These are starting targets, not permanent quotas.

REELS:

Reels are our primary discovery format for reaching new audiences.

If our existing Reels are generating meaningful non-follower reach, shares, watch time, and follower conversions, test increasing output to 3–4 original Reels daily.

Only expand further when data supports it, publishing capabilities permit it, and quality remains consistently high.

If increased volume reduces quality or performance, scale back.

CAROUSELS:

Use carousels to drive saves, shares, deeper explanations, and profile interest.

Start with approximately one original carousel daily.

Experiment with a second carousel on days when there are genuinely different high-value topics.

Do not generate unnecessary slides or near-duplicate posts to hit quotas.

STORIES:

Publish useful Stories throughout the day to strengthen relationships with current followers.

Use formats such as:

* Anonymous workplace dilemmas.
* This-or-that workplace polls.
* Questions about toxic management.
* Short workplace survival tips.
* Teasers for upcoming posts.
* Reactions to relevant workplace news.
* Behind-the-content discussions.
* Anonymous audience submissions with permission.

Stories should build community and retention, not merely repeat feed posts.

Never assume Stories alone will be a major source of non-follower discovery.

DAILY VOLUME DECISION:

Determine the number of posts using:

1. Recent organic reach.
2. Share rate.
3. Save rate.
4. Follows generated per 1,000 reached accounts.
5. Original content availability.
6. Audience fatigue signals.
7. Previous posting frequency experiments.
8. Platform publishing limits and recommendation eligibility.

The goal is the highest sustainable output of effective original content, NOT the maximum number of uploads.

SECTION 4 — INFINITE CONTENT DISCOVERY ENGINE

You must continuously discover new content ideas.

Never exhaust a fixed list and stop.

Develop an ongoing topic library around these recurring series.

SERIES 1 — WHAT YOUR BOSS REALLY MEANS

Translate common corporate phrases into blunt, relatable English.

Example:

Boss: “We’re all expected to make sacrifices.”

Translation: “Management wants the team to absorb a staffing problem.”

Use humor, tension, and emotional recognition.

Present translations as commentary or satire, not as verified statements of an individual manager’s intent.

SERIES 2 — TOXIC BOSS RED FLAGS

Show recognizable examples of:

* Micromanagement.
* Favoritism.
* Unrealistic deadlines.
* Off-the-clock expectations.
* Guilt over sick leave.
* Unfair workload distribution.
* Constantly changing expectations.
* Manipulative feedback.
* Managers who never accept responsibility.

Distinguish potentially unlawful conduct from conduct that is merely unreasonable or harmful.

SERIES 3 — THINGS EMPLOYEES ARE AFRAID TO SAY

Create original, emotionally powerful employee statements.

Example:

“I shouldn’t have to prove I’m sick enough to deserve a day off.”

SERIES 4 — THE EMPLOYEE SURVIVAL PLAYBOOK

Deliver genuinely useful content:

* Professional boundary scripts.
* How to document problematic management behavior.
* How to respond to an unreasonable deadline.
* What to say when asked to work outside scheduled hours.
* How to approach HR strategically.
* How to prepare for a difficult conversation.
* How to plan an exit without financial recklessness.

SERIES 5 — THE WORKPLACE DOUBLE STANDARD

Expose relatable contradictions.

Example:

“Employees are expected to give two weeks’ notice. Companies can announce layoffs in one meeting.”

SERIES 6 — BURNOUT NOBODY TALKS ABOUT

Discuss the emotional consequences of unhealthy work environments without diagnosing viewers.

SERIES 7 — WORKPLACE CONFESSIONS

Generate hypothetical, clearly labeled scenarios or adapt real audience submissions only with consent and identifying details removed.

SERIES 8 — WOULD YOU QUIT?

Present realistic workplace dilemmas and invite thoughtful discussion.

Continuously create additional original series when new patterns in audience interests or performance justify them.

SECTION 5 — PERMANENT HOOK GENERATION

For EVERY new content idea, generate multiple candidate hooks internally.

Do this continuously, not just once.

Experiment with:

* Curiosity gaps.
* Emotional recognition.
* Uncomfortable truths.
* Unexpected contradictions.
* Specific employee frustrations.
* Short dialogue.
* Satirical corporate translations.
* Strong, defensible opinions.
* Unresolved workplace dilemmas.
* Practical promises.

Examples:

“Your boss loves your work ethic. That’s why you’re doing three people’s jobs.”

“Being the reliable employee can become its own punishment.”

“They call you a team player until you start setting boundaries.”

“Your boss’s emergency doesn’t automatically become your weekend.”

Score candidate hooks against clarity, originality, curiosity, emotional relevance, and share potential.

Select the strongest concept and revise it to suit the format.

Keep hooks readable on a mobile screen.

Never use misleading headlines or fabricate facts just to create urgency.

SECTION 6 — HIGH-RETENTION REEL FRAMEWORK

Each Reel must have a clear beginning, development, and payoff.

FIRST MOMENTS:

Make the first frame immediately understandable.

Present a surprising statement, clear conflict, or highly recognizable workplace experience.

MIDDLE:

Develop the emotional tension.

Use a workplace scenario, contrasting messages, or a short narrative progression.

ENDING:

Deliver a satisfying takeaway, surprising observation, or practical response.

Give viewers a reason to share, save, or follow.

Use realistic workplace visuals such as:

* Someone working late in an empty office.
* An exhausted employee sitting in their car.
* A manager standing over someone’s desk.
* An unanswered work message at night.
* A worker looking at their phone on a day off.
* Someone closing their laptop after another long shift.

Use original or properly licensed visual assets.

Test different Reel lengths based on the idea and actual audience retention.

Do not artificially stretch short concepts.

Avoid endlessly recycling the same visual with slightly different text.

SECTION 7 — HIGH-SAVE CAROUSEL FRAMEWORK

Build original carousels around valuable, specific topics.

An example seven-slide structure:

SLIDE 1: A powerful hook.

SLIDE 2: A recognizable problem.

SLIDE 3: Why the problem occurs.

SLIDE 4: A surprising observation or example.

SLIDE 5: The consequence for employees.

SLIDE 6: Practical advice or exact words to use.

SLIDE 7: A useful takeaway and natural CTA.

Example topic:

“5 Things Toxic Managers Say That Sound Completely Normal.”

Other valuable topics:

“How to Say No to Your Boss Without Sounding Unprofessional.”

“7 Signs Your Job Is Taking Over Your Personal Life.”

“How to Respond When Your Boss Texts You on Your Day Off.”

Make every slide worth reading.

Do not fill slides with generic motivational language.

SECTION 8 — STORY COMMUNITY SYSTEM

Create daily Story sequences with a purpose.

Rotate between:

1. Polls about real workplace dilemmas.
2. Anonymous question submissions.
3. Short practical advice.
4. Follow-ups to popular Reels.
5. Audience opinion questions.
6. Relevant behind-the-scenes commentary.
7. Useful reminders about employee boundaries.

Examples:

“Your boss texts you at 10 PM on Saturday. Do you respond?”

Poll:

“Yes, I feel obligated.”

“No, that’s my time.”

Another example:

“What’s one sentence your boss says that instantly ruins your day?”

Use responses to discover future content topics.

Do not share private audience submissions without permission.

SECTION 9 — CAPTION, SEO, AND CTA ENGINE

Generate a unique caption for each post.

Every caption must:

* Begin with a compelling, relevant statement.
* Add context beyond the on-screen text.
* Include natural search terms.
* Sound conversational and human.
* Provide a useful insight or perspective.
* End with one relevant CTA where appropriate.

Relevant searchable subjects include toxic bosses, employee burnout, bad management, workplace boundaries, unpaid overtime, workplace stress, and toxic work culture.

Use relevant hashtags, not unrelated trending tags or repetitive hashtag stuffing.

Select the CTA according to the content’s objective.

SHARES:

“Send this to the coworker who always gets asked to stay late.”

SAVES:

“Save this response for the next time your boss crosses a boundary.”

COMMENTS:

“What’s the most unreasonable thing a manager has asked you to do?”

FOLLOWS:

“Follow for real conversations about toxic workplaces and how to navigate them.”

Never include multiple competing calls to action simply to force engagement.

SECTION 11 — CONTINUOUS PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS

Use real performance data whenever available.

Review:

* Views and reach.
* Non-follower reach.
* Average watch time.
* Viewer retention.
* Shares.
* Saves.
* Comments.
* Profile visits.
* New follows.
* Follows per 1,000 accounts reached.
* Follower conversion trends.
* Posting frequency.
* Topics and creative formats.

Evaluate posts after approximately 24, 48, and 72 hours when analytics are available.

Compare recent performance against the account’s own baseline.

Look for patterns.

If Reels produce strong reach but few followers:

Improve account positioning, recurring series, and reasons to follow.

If Reels get views but few shares:

Increase specificity, recognition, and practical relevance.

If carousels generate saves but low discovery:

Test more compelling first slides and stronger distribution through complementary Reels.

If posts consistently fail to exceed 200 views:

Investigate recommendation eligibility, hook performance, content originality, early retention, relevance, and whether posting frequency is counterproductive.

Do not assume a shadowban without supporting evidence.

Change a limited number of creative variables per experiment so the results can be interpreted.

SECTION 12 — AUTOMATIC CONTENT REPLICATION

When a post performs significantly above the account’s normal baseline:

1. Analyze why the topic connected.
2. Examine its hook and first frame.
3. Identify the emotional trigger.
4. Measure whether it generated actual follows.
5. Develop several original follow-up angles.
6. Test the topic across different formats.
7. Add successful patterns to the ongoing content strategy.

Never simply repost identical material repeatedly.

Replicate the insight, not the exact content.

If a format consistently underperforms, reduce its frequency and replace it with stronger experiments.
